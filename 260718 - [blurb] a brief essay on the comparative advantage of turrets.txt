Naval artillery has often been interpreted as a technological progression from fixed broadsides to rotating turrets, but this obscures a more fundamental question: who controls the geometry of the engagement? The value of a turret is not inherent in its ability to rotate, but in the degree of uncertainty it mitigates. A galley, propelled by oars, possessed extraordinary maneuverability relative to the short ranges at which it fought; its fixed forward weapons were effectively omnidirectional because the hull itself could rapidly determine the orientation of combat. The sailing ship-of-the-line occupied a different regime. Dependent on wind and operating within disciplined formations, it externalized geometric control to fleet doctrine, making the broadside an exceptionally weight-efficient solution. Yet this was not universal. In the archipelagos and littoral waters of Southeast Asia, swivel-mounted lantaka flourished because combat bearings changed rapidly, demonstrating that directional flexibility had long been valued where the battlespace demanded it. Likewise, coastal batteries remained overwhelmingly casemated, not because traverse was technologically unavailable, but because the coastline itself constrained an attacker's approach. Their strategic purpose was less to destroy fleets outright than to deny favorable routes, forcing prolonged operations that ultimately exposed them to the domains in which they were weakest: amphibious assault and air power.

Industrial naval warfare shifted this balance by coupling long-range artillery with steam propulsion, yet the rotating turret should be understood as one solution among many rather than the inevitable culmination of naval architecture. Its immense weight lies not principally in the gun barrels but in the machinery, armor, and ammunition systems required to preserve firepower regardless of bearing, a trade that becomes worthwhile only when engagement geometry cannot be reliably imposed by doctrine, terrain, or maneuver. If reconnaissance, communications, and operational planning instead restore predictability—as through persistent aerial observation over open seas—the broadside recovers much of its historical efficiency, raising the question of whether turret machinery always justifies its displacement. This perspective suggests a conceptual affinity between the ancient galley and the long-range battery cruiser. Both seek to preserve favorable geometry through superior positional freedom: the former through local maneuverability, the latter through strategic mobility and engagement ranges beyond an opponent's effective reply. Such a doctrine remains vulnerable when information superiority is lost or the battlespace is compressed, as in hostile coastal waters, but it illustrates a broader principle. Throughout naval history, the decisive issue has rarely been whether guns traversed, but where maneuverability resided—within the hull, the weapon, the fleet, the terrain, or the operational system that bound them together.
